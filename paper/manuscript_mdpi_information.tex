\Title{Prequential Answer-Set Calibration for Temporal Knowledge Graph Forecasting}

\Author{Xinyu Wang $^{1,*}$}
\AuthorNames{Xinyu Wang}
\address{%
$^{1}$ \quad Anhui Institute of Information Technology, Wuhu 241000, China}
\corres{Correspondence: xywang68@iflytek.com}

\abstract{Forecasts from temporal knowledge graphs can inform event monitoring and downstream decisions, yet standard ranking metrics do not indicate whether a returned answer set covers the recorded truth at a stated rate. Existing conformal methods for knowledge graphs mainly calibrate static link predictions. Can calibration use only previously revealed timestamp batches to repair reliability under temporal ordering, and what utility does that repair cost? We study this question with a leakage-controlled prequential protocol rather than claim validity under arbitrary drift. The evaluation crosses ICEWS14 and ICEWS05-15, two matched-protocol scoring families, five seeds, four score transformations, three history policies, controlled deletion of training facts, and filtered-negative sensitivity. At 30\% deletion and a 0.90 target, rolling margin calibration attains 0.8988--0.9002 observed-label coverage. Relative to a fixed static margin threshold, it gains 0.0798--0.1025 coverage but adds 1158--2038 entities per set. A factorial ablation shows that history and score geometry interact: rolling Minmax saves 1091--1773 entities relative to rolling margin, with dataset-dependent coverage effects, whereas rolling Softmax overcovers and adds 2081--4991 entities. Training-fact deletion and the tested negative-sampling choice do not explain the main reliability gap. Multi-answer full-set coverage remains markedly below single-answer coverage, and a query-max calibration objective further reduces full-set coverage by 0.0011--0.0081. Recent-history calibration is therefore a useful temporal reliability diagnostic, but not a uniformly efficient or universally valid solution.}

\keyword{temporal knowledge graph; link forecasting; conformal prediction; prequential evaluation; uncertainty quantification; answer sets; temporal shift}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Temporal knowledge graphs (TKGs) encode time-stamped relations among entities.
They support forecasts of future interactions and events in settings where the
cost of overlooking a plausible answer may exceed the cost of inspecting a
short list. Most TKG models are nevertheless evaluated with filtered mean
reciprocal rank (MRR) and Hits@\(K\)
\cite{garciaduran2018sequence,li2021regcn,li2022tirgn,cai2023survey}. These
metrics assess ordering. They do not answer a different operational question:
does a set returned with nominal coverage \(1-\alpha\) actually contain the
recorded correct entity at that rate?

Conformal prediction turns model scores into prediction sets without requiring
a parametric label-probability model
\cite{vovk2005algorithmic,angelopoulos2023gentle}. KGCP applies split conformal
prediction to static knowledge graph embeddings, and CondKGCP studies
predicate-conditional answer sets \cite{zhu2025kgcp,zhu2025condkgcp}. Temporal
forecasting changes the information structure. Calibration precedes the test
stream, score distributions may evolve, and a label observed at timestamp
\(t\) must not influence another prediction made for the same timestamp. A
fixed threshold may therefore cease to be reliable even when the ranking model
itself is unchanged.

This paper asks: \emph{can an answer-set threshold updated only from strictly
past timestamp batches repair temporal reliability, and what is paid in set
size and subgroup behavior?} We answer with a timestamp-batched prequential
protocol. A frozen scorer first emits every subject- and object-side prediction
at time \(t\). Only then are labels from that batch released to the calibration
history. Static, expanding, and recent rolling histories are crossed with four
nonconformity scores so that the effect of history is not confused with the
effect of score geometry.

The evidence is broader than a single-model case study but remains deliberately
controlled. The main matrix covers ICEWS14 and ICEWS05-15, temporal DistMult
and a matched-protocol continuous-time complex scorer, five seeds, and deletion
of up to 30\% of training facts. Paired sensitivity runs compare filtered and
uniform training negatives. A follow-up factorial analysis reuses verified
checkpoints and evaluates four scores under three history policies. In total,
the primary matrix contains 90 completed seed--deletion conditions, while the
factorial analysis contains 20 dataset--scorer--seed cases and 131,550
timestamp--method rows.

Three findings shape the contribution. First, a recent-history margin threshold
repairs the severe undercoverage of a fixed margin threshold in all four
dataset--scorer cases, but requires much larger sets. Second, the best
reliability--utility trade-off is not stable across score functions or datasets:
Minmax is markedly smaller on ICEWS14, whereas Softmax becomes inefficiently
conservative under rolling updates. Third, marginal label coverage conceals a
large multi-answer deficit, and the most direct query-max calibration attempt
does not correct it. The contribution is a leakage-controlled diagnostic, a
factorized empirical account of its failure modes, and an explicit boundary on
what temporal conformal claims the evidence can support. It is not a new
distribution-free theorem for arbitrary temporal drift.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Temporal knowledge graph forecasting.}
TKG completion methods include temporal sequence encoders, tensor
factorizations, and recurrent graph networks
\cite{garciaduran2018sequence,lacroix2020tcomplex,li2021regcn,li2022tirgn}.
Simple historical baselines can remain competitive, which makes split design
and temporal information control central to evaluation
\cite{gastinger2024repeat,gastinger2024tgb2}. Our calibration layer consumes a
frozen all-entity score vector and does not change candidate ranking. The two
scorer families used below are therefore controlled protocol variants, not new
forecasting architectures.

\textbf{Conformal prediction for graph outputs.}
Probability calibration has been studied for neural classifiers and knowledge
graph embeddings \cite{guo2017calibration,safavi2020calibration}. KGCP constructs
answer sets for link prediction with NegScore, query-wise Minmax, and Softmax
transformations \cite{zhu2025kgcp}; CondKGCP moves from marginal toward
predicate-conditional behavior \cite{zhu2025condkgcp}. APS and RAPS use ranked
probability mass to trade coverage against set size in multiclass prediction
\cite{angelopoulos2021raps}. These studies make score design a first-order
experimental factor rather than a cosmetic implementation choice.

CFEP recently addressed conformal event-type prediction on temporal knowledge
graphs with score diffusion and learned quantile weights \cite{hu2026cfep}.
Our estimand and protocol differ. We study entity answer sets for link
forecasting, freeze the scorer, and isolate how past-only history and score
geometry affect calibration. The aim is diagnostic: the analysis reports where
a simple temporal update works, what it costs, and where it fails.

\textbf{Calibration under non-exchangeability.}
Weighted conformal prediction, Adaptive Conformal Inference, and analyses of
non-exchangeable data provide guarantees under specified shift or online
feedback conditions
\cite{tibshirani2019covariate,gibbs2021adaptive,barber2023beyond,farinhas2024nexcrc}.
NCPNET studies non-exchangeable temporal graph neural networks
\cite{wang2025ncpnet}. Our rolling rule is intentionally simpler: it estimates
an empirical quantile from the most recent revealed scores. Because it uses no
likelihood-ratio weights and assumes no bounded drift process, exchangeable
coverage cannot simply be transferred to the test stream.

\section{Problem, Calibration, and Guarantee Boundary}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Temporal Queries and Reliability Targets}

Let \(\mathcal{E}\) and \(\mathcal{R}\) be closed entity and relation
vocabularies. A TKG contains quadruples \((s,r,o,t)\). We write an object query
as \(x=(s,r,\square,t)\), where \(\square\) denotes the entity slot to be
predicted, and represent subject prediction by \((o,r^{-1},\square,t)\). For a
query \(x\), let \(A_t(x)\subseteq\mathcal{E}\) be all recorded answers at
timestamp \(t\), and let \(C_t(x)\) be the predicted answer set.

The primary reliability estimand is observed-label coverage,
\[
  \widehat{\cov}_{\mathrm{label}}
  =\frac{1}{N}\sum_{(x,y)}\mathbb{1}\{y\in C_t(x)\}.
\]
It differs from full-set query coverage,
\(\mathbb{1}\{A_t(x)\subseteq C_t(x)\}\). We report both, together with mean
set size, filtered MRR, and partial answer recall
\(|A_t(x)\cap C_t(x)|/|A_t(x)|\). Each quantity refers to labels recorded in
the benchmark, not to every event that might be true outside the data.

\subsection{Scores and History Policies}

Let \(f_\theta(x,y)\) be the score assigned by a frozen TKG model. Margin
nonconformity is
\[
 a_{\mathrm{margin}}(x,y)=\max_{z\in\mathcal{E}}f_\theta(x,z)-f_\theta(x,y),
 \qquad C_q(x)=\{y:a(x,y)\le q\}.
 \tag{1}\label{eq:margin}
\]
For calibration scores \(a_1,\ldots,a_n\) and target \(1-\alpha\), let
\[
 k=\min\{n,\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil\},\qquad q=a_{(k)}.
 \tag{2}\label{eq:quantile}
\]
Static history estimates \(q\) once from the final initial-calibration interval.
Expanding history uses every score revealed before the current timestamp.
Rolling history uses the most recent \(W=1000\) revealed scores.

To separate the score from the history, the factorial study also evaluates the
three transformations used by KGCP. For score vector \(f\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
 a_{\mathrm{Neg}}(y)&=-f_y,\\
 a_{\mathrm{Minmax}}(y)&=-\frac{f_y-\min_z f_z}{\max_z f_z-\min_z f_z},\\
 a_{\mathrm{Softmax}}(y)&=1-\frac{\exp(f_y/T)}{\sum_z\exp(f_z/T)},
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}\label{eq:scores}
\]
where zero-range Minmax vectors map to zero and \(T=1\). We reproduce these
published score definitions under our common split and scorers; we do not claim
to reproduce the complete KGCP training pipeline.

At timestamp \(t\), all query scores, thresholds, and sets are stored before
any label from \(t\) is released. The entire batch is then appended to the
expanding and rolling histories. This ordering prevents within-timestamp
leakage.

\subsection{Coverage Bounds and Set Geometry}

The first theorem states the familiar exchangeable benchmark against which the
temporal experiment should be read. It is included to make clear which part of
the usual conformal argument is lost after a chronological split
\cite{vovk2005algorithmic,angelopoulos2023gentle}.

\noindent\textbf{Theorem 1 (exchangeable split-conformal reference).}\quad
Condition on a frozen scorer. Suppose \(a_1,\ldots,a_n,a_{n+1}\) are
exchangeable and \(\alpha\ge 1/(n+1)\). With \(q\) from
Equation~\eqref{eq:quantile},
\[
 \Pr\{a_{n+1}\le q\}\ge 1-\alpha.
 \tag{4}\label{eq:exchangeable}
\]
If the scores are almost surely distinct, the coverage is at most
\(1-\alpha+1/(n+1)\).

\emph{Proof.}
Exchangeability makes the rank of \(a_{n+1}\) among the \(n+1\) scores uniform.
The event that this rank does not exceed
\(\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil\) has probability at least \(1-\alpha\).
Using the calibration order statistic can only include tied test scores; without
ties, the next attainable rank contributes at most \(1/(n+1)\). \hfill\(\square\)

Temporal ordering need not satisfy the premise of Theorem~1. The next result
does not restore that guarantee; it decomposes the error of an empirical
threshold. Condition on all information available before time \(t\). Let
\(F_t\) be the CDF of the current observed-label score, \(\widehat H_t\) the
empirical CDF of the selected past-score pool, and \(H_t\) a reference CDF for
that pool. Define
\[
 \delta_t=\sup_u|F_t(u)-H_t(u)|,\qquad
 \epsilon_t=\sup_u|\widehat H_t(u)-H_t(u)|.
\]
Let \(\eta_t=\widehat H_t(q_t)-\widehat H_t(q_t^-)\) be the empirical mass tied
at the selected threshold, and
\(\rho_{n,\alpha}=\max\{0,(k-1)/n-(1-\alpha)\}\).

\noindent\textbf{Theorem 2 (deterministic temporal-mismatch decomposition).}\quad
For the inclusive threshold in Equation~\eqref{eq:quantile},
\[
 1-\alpha-\delta_t-\epsilon_t
 \le F_t(q_t)\le
 1-\alpha+\rho_{n,\alpha}+\eta_t+\delta_t+\epsilon_t,
 \tag{5}\label{eq:decomposition}
\]
with both bounds clipped to \([0,1]\).

\emph{Proof.}
By construction, \(\widehat H_t(q_t)\ge k/n\ge1-\alpha\). Two applications of
the sup-norm bounds yield the lower inequality. Immediately before \(q_t\), at
most \(k-1\) empirical observations are strictly smaller, so
\(\widehat H_t(q_t)\le(k-1)/n+\eta_t\). Applying the same two deviations gives
the upper inequality. \hfill\(\square\)

The decomposition identifies a trade-off. A shorter history may reduce temporal
mismatch \(\delta_t\), yet increase empirical error \(\epsilon_t\) or the effect
of atoms \(\eta_t\). These terms are not estimated as a new guarantee; the
experiments measure their observable consequence through coverage and set size.

\noindent\textbf{Proposition 1 (set nesting and multi-answer criterion).}\quad
For any nonconformity score, \(q_1\le q_2\) implies
\(C_{q_1}(x)\subseteq C_{q_2}(x)\). For margin nonconformity and \(q\ge0\), the
set is nonempty. Moreover,
\[
 A_t(x)\subseteq C_q(x)
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \max_{y\in A_t(x)}a(x,y)\le q.
 \tag{6}\label{eq:querymax}
\]

\emph{Proof.}
Prediction sets are nonconformity sublevel sets and therefore nested. Under
Equation~\eqref{eq:margin}, a top-scoring entity has score zero. The final
equivalence follows because every recorded answer is included exactly when its
largest nonconformity score does not exceed the threshold. \hfill\(\square\)

Equation~\eqref{eq:querymax} motivates a query-max calibration objective. It
does not imply that its empirical threshold will dominate a label-level
threshold: changing from labels to unique queries changes both the sample size
and the order statistic. We test that distinction directly.

\section{Experiments and Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Study Design}

The experiments answer four questions. \textbf{RQ1} asks whether recent-history
calibration repairs static undercoverage across datasets and scorers.
\textbf{RQ2} separates history policy from score geometry and measures their
utility cost. \textbf{RQ3} tests whether training-fact deletion or filtered
negative sampling explains the main effect. \textbf{RQ4} measures multi-answer
reliability and tests the query-max objective from Proposition~1.

ICEWS14 and ICEWS05-15 are event graphs derived from the ICEWS coded event data
\cite{boschee2015icews}. Table~\ref{tab:data} reports the normalized graph
sizes. Unique timestamps are sorted: the earliest 60\% train the scorer, the
next 20\% provide disjoint validation and calibration roles, and the final
20\% form the test stream. The middle interval is divided chronologically into
25\% scorer validation, 30\% calibrator tuning, 15\% selector validation, and
30\% final initial calibration.

\centering
\caption{Normalized dataset statistics. Relations exclude inverse copies used
internally for subject prediction.\label{tab:data}}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Dataset & Entities & Relations & Facts & Timestamps\\
\midrule
ICEWS14 & 7,128 & 230 & 90,730 & 365\\
ICEWS05-15 & 10,488 & 251 & 461,329 & 4,017\\

Temporal DistMult uses 256-dimensional real embeddings. The second scorer uses
128-dimensional complex embeddings and a shared continuous-time map formed by
constant, linear, quadratic, sine, and cosine features. It is a
matched-protocol ComplEx-style alternative, not the official TComplEx or
TNTComplEx optimizer. Both scorers use the same pairwise softplus objective,
Adam, learning rate \(10^{-3}\), weight decay \(10^{-6}\), batch size 1024,
128 negatives, at most 200 epochs, and validation-based early stopping.

The primary matrix uses filtered training negatives and seeds
\(\{17,29,43,59,71\}\). ICEWS14 uses both scorers at deletion rates
\(\{0,0.1,0.2,0.3\}\). ICEWS05-15 uses all four rates for DistMult and rates
\(\{0,0.3\}\) for the continuous complex scorer. Deletion affects training
facts only and retains an entity's earliest occurrence if uniform deletion
would otherwise remove all of its training occurrences. Paired
uniform-negative sensitivity is run for ICEWS05-15 DistMult and ICEWS14
continuous complex at deletion rates 0 and 0.3. Filtered negatives exclude
positives retained in the training graph and do not consult future facts.

The factorial follow-up fixes deletion at 0.3, uses filtered negatives, and
reuses the verified checkpoint for each of the four dataset--scorer cases and
five seeds. It crosses the four scores in Equation~\eqref{eq:scores} with
static, expanding, and rolling histories. It also evaluates query-max margin
under the same three histories. No test result selects a score or policy.

The target coverage is 0.90. Primary intervals use 20,000 equal-seed circular
moving-block bootstrap replicates with seven-timestamp blocks; block lengths 3,
14, and 21 test sensitivity to the dependence scale. Contrasts are paired by
seed and timestamp. Code, resolved configurations, derived result tables,
figure scripts, and checksums are available at
\url{https://github.com/xywang815/riskcal-tkg-w}. Raw ICEWS data are available
from \url{https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/28075} and the public benchmark source
documented in the repository.

\subsection{RQ1: Recent History Repairs Static Margin Undercoverage}

Figure~\ref{fig:generalization} first establishes the cross-dataset pattern.
At 30\% deletion, rolling margin coverage is 0.8996 and 0.8988 on ICEWS14 with
DistMult and the continuous complex scorer, and 0.9002 for both scorers on
ICEWS05-15. The paired rolling-minus-static gains are 0.0798 (95\% CI
[0.0621, 0.1006]), 0.0802 ([0.0612, 0.1014]), 0.1011
([0.0921, 0.1102]), and 0.1025 ([0.0938, 0.1111]), respectively.
The corresponding filtered MRR values are 0.3103, 0.3129, 0.3269, and 0.3295;
calibration changes answer-set membership but leaves this ranking unchanged.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/expansion/expansion_generalization.pdf}
\caption{Observed-label coverage across deletion rates. Points are means over
five seeds, error bars show one seed standard deviation, and the dashed line is
the 0.90 target. ICEWS05-15 continuous complex is evaluated at deletion rates
0 and 0.3. Methods share the same scorer within each panel.\label{fig:generalization}}
\end{figure}

The repair is not free. In the factorized follow-up, rolling margin increases
mean set size over static margin by 1175 entities on ICEWS14 DistMult (95\% CI
[959, 1416]), 1158 on ICEWS14 continuous complex ([947, 1382]), 2026 on
ICEWS05-15 DistMult ([1867, 2183]), and 2038 on ICEWS05-15 continuous complex
([1888, 2182]). The absolute target-error reduction is positive for every case
and every tested block length, as is the set-size increase. The reliable part of
the conclusion is therefore a paired movement toward the target accompanied by
a substantial loss of efficiency.

\subsection{RQ2: History and Score Geometry Interact}

Figure~\ref{fig:factorial} separates the two components that the initial study
coupled. Margin and Softmax change sharply as the history moves from static to
expanding to rolling. NegScore and Minmax are comparatively stable on ICEWS14,
where their static thresholds already lie near 0.90. On ICEWS05-15, all four
scores respond to the history choice, but they occupy different regions of the
coverage--size plane.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/followup/followup_factorial.pdf}
\caption{Factorial ablation of nonconformity score and calibration history at
30\% deletion. Each colored path connects static (square), expanding
(triangle), and rolling-1000 (circle) histories for one score. The dashed line
is the 0.90 target. Values are equal-seed means; paired uncertainty is reported
in the text.\label{fig:factorial}}
\end{figure}

Within rolling history, Minmax changes ICEWS14 coverage by only 0.0001 and
0.0005 relative to margin, with both 95\% intervals crossing zero, while
reducing mean set size by 1773 and 1650 entities. On ICEWS05-15 it reduces size
by 1187 and 1091 entities, but its coverage changes of -0.0014 and -0.0011 are
small and sensitive to block length. NegScore shows the same dataset dependence:
it is smaller without a resolved coverage change on ICEWS14, but slightly less
reliable and not consistently smaller on ICEWS05-15.

Softmax illustrates why coverage alone is insufficient. Under rolling history,
it raises coverage over margin by 0.069--0.085, yet adds 2081--4991 entities.
The resulting coverage of 0.9683--0.9854 is overconservative and leaves little
screening value on the larger graph. No score function dominates across all
cases; score geometry and temporal history must be assessed jointly.

A separate ICEWS14 DistMult shortlist diagnostic reached the same caution from
a different construction. A validation-selected RAPS-style rolling rule used
softmax temperature \(T=1\), selection tolerance 0.02, and a fixed 21-candidate
grid comprising APS plus
\(k\in\{50,100,250,500,1000\}\) and
\(\lambda\in\{10^{-4},5\times10^{-4},10^{-3},2\times10^{-3}\}\).
All 20 seed--deletion conditions selected \(k=50\) and
\(\lambda=10^{-4}\). At 30\% deletion it reduced mean set size from 4069.4 for
rolling margin to 3319.0 while changing observed-label coverage from 0.8998 to
0.8987; full-set query coverage fell from 0.9020 to 0.8874. This is an
efficiency diagnostic under one scorer, not evidence of a general improvement.

\subsection{RQ3: The Tested Data Interventions Do Not Explain the Gap}

Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics} distinguishes the history effect from two proposed
mechanisms. For 30\% minus 0\% deletion, every static-margin and rolling-margin
coverage interval crosses zero. Static changes range from -0.0024 to -0.0004,
and rolling changes range from 0.0000 to 0.0005. Training-fact deletion is thus
a scorer stress test, but the experiment does not support it as the cause of
static calibration failure.

Filtered-minus-uniform rolling effects also cross zero in all four paired
conditions. A small positive static-margin effect appears only for ICEWS05-15
DistMult without deletion (0.0020 [0.0005, 0.0038]); the remaining static
intervals are inconclusive. The main history result is not attributable to the
tested negative-sampling choice.

\subsection{RQ4: Label Coverage Conceals Multi-Answer Failure}

In the factorial follow-up, under rolling label-margin calibration,
multi-answer full-set coverage is lower than single-answer coverage in every
case: the mean gaps are -0.2773 and
-0.3015 on ICEWS14, and -0.2059 and -0.2137 on ICEWS05-15, for DistMult and
continuous complex respectively. Marginal label coverage near 0.90 therefore
does not imply that a set contains all recorded answers to a query.

Proposition~1 suggests calibrating the maximum nonconformity over each recorded
answer set. Empirically, that direct query-max rule does not solve the problem
(Figure~\ref{fig:queryobjective}). Relative to label-level rolling margin, its
full-set coverage changes by -0.0081 (95\% CI [-0.0107, -0.0058]) and -0.0070
([-0.0093, -0.0049]) on ICEWS14. The ICEWS05-15 changes are -0.0011
([-0.0022, 0.0001]) and -0.0014 ([-0.0024, -0.0003]). Sets shrink by 39--141
entities, but the multi-answer gap becomes more negative. Reducing the number
of calibration units from labels to unique queries changes the empirical
quantile enough to offset the apparently conservative maximum score.

\section{Discussion and Conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central result is narrower, and more useful, than a universal method claim.
When a margin threshold is fixed early, coverage later in the chronological
test stream falls to roughly 0.80--0.82. Re-estimating that threshold from the
most recent strictly past scores restores aggregate observed-label coverage
near 0.90 in both ICEWS datasets and both controlled scorer families. Theorem~2
offers the corresponding diagnostic interpretation: recent history can reduce
the discrepancy between the past-score reference and the current score
distribution. The data do not identify that discrepancy term directly, and no
coverage guarantee under arbitrary drift follows.

The factorial ablation changes how this result should be used. Rolling updates
are decisive for margin, but score transformation can matter as much as history.
Minmax is far more efficient on ICEWS14, while its ICEWS05-15 coverage effect is
mixed. Rolling Softmax obtains higher coverage by returning most of the entity
vocabulary. A deployment rule should therefore be chosen from a joint
reliability--utility frontier, not from coverage or mean set size alone.

Two negative findings further limit the explanation. Removing up to 30\% of
training facts does not measurably worsen static coverage, and filtered training
negatives have little effect in the paired sensitivity study. Temporal mismatch,
rather than the tested graph corruption, is the defensible motivation for
updating the threshold. The multi-answer analysis also exposes a different
estimand failure: label-marginal calibration may be adequate while the event
that all answers are present remains unreliable. A simple query-max score is
not enough because its calibration sample and quantile change at the same time.

The study has four main boundaries. ICEWS14 and ICEWS05-15 are both political
event graphs, so they do not establish domain-wide generality. The second
scorer is a matched-protocol continuous complex model rather than an official
TComplEx or TNTComplEx reproduction. That distinction is necessary because the
published TKBC preprocessing places the same discrete time support in training,
validation, and test, whereas the present protocol requires unseen future
timestamps; a model with a free embedding for every future time would either be
untrained there or leak temporal information. Likewise, the KGCP comparisons
reuse published score transformations, not the authors' full training pipeline.
Finally, five seeds limit inference over optimization randomness, and a moving-
block bootstrap only approximates the dependence structure.

These limits point to concrete next tests. Temporal answer-set calibration
should be evaluated with inductive or extrapolative TKG backbones on non-ICEWS
domains. Structured calibration objectives should preserve query-level sample
size or explicitly model answer multiplicity. Predicate-conditional temporal
rules may reduce the large relation and direction heterogeneity that marginal
coverage averages away. For large entity vocabularies, any reliability gain
must also be paired with candidate-generation or retrieval methods that bound
the cost of all-entity scoring.

In conclusion, recent-history prequential calibration repairs a reproducible
aggregate undercoverage pattern, but it does so with thousands of additional
candidates and leaves multi-answer reliability unresolved. The practical
contribution is a protocol for auditing that trade-off without current-timestamp
leakage, together with evidence showing which apparently plausible explanations
and remedies do not survive controlled tests.
